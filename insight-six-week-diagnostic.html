The Six-Week Diagnostic Is Already Obsolete

How AI collapsed the timeline on serious business decisions — and why paying for months of analysis now costs you more than the analysis is worth.

There is a ritual at the heart of traditional consulting, and most owner-operators have paid for it at least once. A brand-name firm arrives, scopes a "diagnostic," and disappears for six to twelve weeks. A team of analysts a year or two out of business school pulls data, runs interviews, and builds a deck. Then the partners return, present a hundred slides, hand you an invoice, and leave you to execute a recommendation you now understand only as well as the deck explains it.

That model made sense in a world where analysis was scarce and expensive. That world is gone.

What actually took six weeks

Strip the romance away and the traditional diagnostic was mostly gathering and formatting — collecting data, normalizing it, building comparison tables, drafting exhibits, and polishing a presentation. The genuinely hard part, the judgment about which questions matter and what the evidence implies, was a fraction of the hours. The rest was production time, billed at premium rates.

AI compresses exactly that production time. The work that filled the first four weeks of a traditional engagement — structuring data, running scenarios, pressure-testing assumptions, drafting the first version of every exhibit — now happens in days. What's left is the judgment, and judgment was never the thing that took six weeks.

Slow is no longer a sign of rigor

The old pitch quietly equated time with thoroughness: a longer engagement felt more serious, more complete, more worth the fee. But a decision doesn't get better because a team sat with it for two months. It gets better when the right options are on the table, the assumptions behind each are tested, and the recommendation traces to evidence instead of a partner's instinct.

None of that requires a calendar quarter. It requires a disciplined process and someone who knows which questions actually decide the outcome. Speed, done right, is not a shortcut around rigor — it's what rigor looks like once the busywork is automated.

The real cost of waiting

For an owner-operator, the six-week diagnostic carries a cost that never appears on the invoice: the decision doesn't move while you wait. The market shifts. A competitor acts. A window closes. You are paying premium rates and carrying the cost of delay, often on a decision that was time-sensitive to begin with.

When the same rigor can be delivered in days, months of waiting stop being a mark of quality and start being a liability you're paying for twice.

What "fast, done right" requires

Speed without discipline is just a guess with better formatting. Compressing the timeline honestly takes three things:

  • A structured method, not a blank page. Every option evaluated on the same terms — value at stake, probability of success, strategic fit, confidence in the evidence — so choices are compared, not argued.
  • AI pointed at the production work, not the judgment. Analysis, scenarios, and first-draft exhibits are accelerated; the decision about what it means stays with experienced people.
  • Evidence you can trace. Every claim tied to a specific source, so a fast answer is also a defensible one.

That is the whole case for the modern model: the rigor the big firms sell, delivered in days instead of months, by people who have actually run things — at a fraction of the price, because you're no longer paying for weeks of formatting.

The question to ask your next consultant

Next time a firm proposes a multi-week diagnostic, ask a simple question: how much of that timeline is judgment, and how much is production? If most of it is production, you're being billed for work a modern process no longer needs to do slowly.

The decision in front of you deserves rigor. It does not require you to wait a quarter — or to pay for the privilege.
